A hiring signal is not the same as hiring intent

A contract award lands. A funding round closes. A careers page starts filling with new roles. From the outside, each can look like the beginning of a recruiting opportunity. Sometimes it is. Sometimes nothing happens at all.

The mistake is treating a public signal as proof of an immediate need. New capital can fund product, inventory, acquisitions, or runway. Government work can be absorbed by an existing team. A job posting can already have a strong internal pipeline.

A signal tells you where to look. It does not tell you what the buyer will say.

I still like signals because they improve the starting point. They show where something has changed: new work, new money, or an explicit opening. That is more useful than contacting every company simply because it exists.

The next step is qualification. Is the role difficult? Is timing real? Is the company open to outside help? What does the person absolutely need to have? Those answers are what turn a market signal into a matchable recruiting need.

The introduction only makes sense after that. A signal narrows the map; the conversation tells you whether there is actually a route between the hiring company and a specialist recruiter.

— I watch public demand signals, then qualify before I route an introduction.
